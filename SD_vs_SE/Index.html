Introduction

In scientific literature, two indices are frequently confused or used as synonyms: the Standard Deviation (SD) and the Standard Error of the Mean (SE). Both are expressed in the same units as the data and often appear in the same places (error bars in graphs, mean ± value in the text), yet they answer fundamentally different questions.

The SD answers the question: "How much do individual subjects vary in my population or sample?"
The SE answers the question: "How precise is my estimate of the population mean?"

Understanding this distinction is not just a technical detail: it is essential for properly interpreting your data, choosing the most honest graph, and applying statistical tests appropriately. In this short guide, we will look at the theory, the formulas, and calculate them in R using real biological data.

Preliminary note

This discussion assumes familiarity with the concepts of sample mean and random sampling. Concepts like sampling distribution, Central Limit Theorem, and t-tests are mentioned but will be covered in dedicated lessons.

Standard Deviation (SD)

Definition

The sample Standard Deviation measures the dispersion of individual values around the sample mean. It tells us how much, on average, each single observation deviates from the group mean. It is an index of the intrinsic variability of the studied phenomenon.

In biology, this index is very common: the variability of wing length in an insect species, the distribution of yield per plant in an agricultural plot, the body weight in a vertebrate population. All these variables have their own SD that describes how "heterogeneous" the individuals in the sample are.

Formula

Sample Standard Deviation $$s = \sqrt{\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}}$$

Where: $x_i$ = value of the $i$-th observation  |  $\bar{x}$ = sample mean  |  $n$ = sample size  |  $n-1$ = degrees of freedom (Bessel's correction)

Why n − 1 and not n?

Dividing by $n-1$ instead of $n$ makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance $\sigma^2$. This happens because the sample mean $\bar{x}$ is estimated from the same data used to calculate the SD, "consuming" one degree of freedom. This aspect will be detailed in a lesson dedicated to estimators.

Main properties

  • It is measured in the same unit as the original data (e.g., cm, g, individuals/trap).
  • It is always non-negative; it is zero only if all values are identical.
  • It does not systematically depend on $n$: with larger samples, the SD converges to the population standard deviation $\sigma$, but it does not systematically decrease.
  • For approximately normal distributions, about 68% of the values fall in the interval $\bar{x} \pm 1 \cdot s$, and about 95% in the interval $\bar{x} \pm 2 \cdot s$.
  • It is sensitive to outliers: an extreme value can considerably inflate the SD.

Practical use

The SD is a descriptive tool. It should be used when you want to characterize the biological variability of a phenomenon, present the distribution of a trait in a population, or define biological reference limits (for example, the "normal" range of a physiological parameter). In figures, error bars representing the SD show where the individuals are located, not the precision of the mean estimate.

  • Short bars: Indicate that the values of your sample are all very close to the mean value. There is low variability and the data is highly "concentrated".
  • Long bars: Indicate that the sample values are very spread out and far from the mean. There is high variability in the observed phenomenon.

Standard Error of the Mean (SE)

Definition

The Standard Error of the Mean estimates the precision with which the sample mean $\bar{x}$ approximates the true population mean $\mu$. Formally, it is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the means: if we repeated the sampling many times and calculated the mean each time, that sequence of means would have its own standard deviation — and that is exactly the SE.

Imagine sampling the petal length of 50 Iris setosa specimens across ten different sites: you would get ten slightly different sample means. The SE quantifies how much these means vary around the true mean of the species.

Formula

Standard Error of the Mean $$SE_{\bar{x}} = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Where: $s$ = sample standard deviation  |  $n$ = sample size

The formula immediately reveals a crucial property: the SE is always smaller than the SD (since $\sqrt{n} > 1$ for $n > 1$), and it decreases proportionally to $1/\sqrt{n}$. To halve the SE, you need to quadruple the sample size.

Main properties

  • It is measured in the same unit as the data, like the SD.
  • It decreases as $n$ increases: the more observations we collect, the more precise the estimate of the mean is.
  • It depends both on the intrinsic variability of the data ($s$) and on the amount of observations ($n$).
  • It provides the basis for confidence intervals: $CI_{95\%} \approx \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot SE$ (for large samples).
  • For small samples, the quantile of Student's t distribution is used: $\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\, n-1} \cdot SE$.

Practical use

The SE is an inferential tool. It should be used when you want to make statements about the population mean, build confidence intervals, or compare the means of different groups (treatments, species, sites, years). In figures, error bars representing the SE show the uncertainty in the estimate of the mean, which is the relevant information for group comparisons.

Comparison between SD and SE

The following table summarizes the key differences. Keeping them in mind greatly facilitates making the right choice in everyday practice.

Aspect Standard Deviation (SD) Standard Error (SE)
What it measures Variability of the individual observed values Precision of the mean estimate
Question it answers "How much do individuals vary?" "How precise is my mean?"
Effect of n Stable (converges to $\sigma$) Decreases as $1/\sqrt{n}$
Type of statistic Descriptive Inferential
Formula $s = \sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2/(n-1)}$ $SE = s/\sqrt{n}$
Error bars in figures To describe the dispersion of the data For comparisons between means of different groups
Mutual relationship SD ≥ SE always SE = SD / $\sqrt{n}$ ≤ SD
Common mistake in scientific literature

Many articles report error bars without specifying whether they represent SD or SE. Since the SE is always smaller than the SD, reporting SE in a descriptive graph makes the data seem much less variable than it is. Some journals mandate the use of the 95% confidence interval precisely for clarity. Always check the figure legend.

Practical Example in R

We use the iris dataset, built into R by default, focusing on the Iris setosa species and the petal length variable (Petal.Length). The sample consists of $n = 50$ measurements expressed in centimeters. It is a simple, biologically real dataset, perfect for concretely illustrating the two indices.

The complete and commented R scripts are available in the R/ folder of the repository and can be downloaded using the buttons in the headers of the gray boxes below.

R Script 01 — Calculating SD and SE · R/01_calcolo_sd_se.R
↓ Download 
# Load built-in R dataset
data(iris)

# Extraction: Iris setosa, petal length
setosa_pl <- iris[iris$Species == "setosa", "Petal.Length"]

# --- Basic parameters ---
n     <- length(setosa_pl)         # sample size
media <- mean(setosa_pl)           # sample mean

# --- Standard Deviation (SD) ---
# sd() uses Bessel's correction (n-1): unbiased estimator
sd_val <- sd(setosa_pl)

# Manual check
sd_manuale <- sqrt(sum((setosa_pl - media)^2) / (n - 1))
# all.equal(sd_val, sd_manuale)  →  TRUE

# --- Standard Error of the Mean (SE) ---
# There is no dedicated function in base R
se_val <- sd_val / sqrt(n)

# --- 95% Confidence Interval ---
t_critico <- qt(0.975, df = n - 1)   # Student's t distribution
ic_low    <- media - t_critico * se_val
ic_high   <- media + t_critico * se_val

# --- Output ---
cat(sprintf("  n                            : %d\n", n))
cat(sprintf("  Mean (x̄)                     : %.4f cm\n", media))
cat(sprintf("  Standard Deviation (SD)      : %.4f cm\n", sd_val))
cat(sprintf("  Standard Error (SE)          : %.4f cm\n", se_val))
cat(sprintf("  Mean ± SD                    : [%.4f – %.4f] cm\n",
            media - sd_val, media + sd_val))
cat(sprintf("  Mean ± SE                    : [%.4f – %.4f] cm\n",
            media - se_val, media + se_val))
cat(sprintf("  95%% CI (Student's t, df=%d)  : [%.4f – %.4f] cm\n",
            n - 1, ic_low, ic_high))
Expected Output n : 50 Mean (x̄) : 1.4620 cm Standard Deviation (SD) : 0.1737 cm Standard Error (SE) : 0.0246 cm Mean ± SD : [1.2883 – 1.6357] cm Mean ± SE : [1.4374 – 1.4866] cm 95% CI (Student's t, df=49) : [1.4126 – 1.5114] cm
R Script 02 — Graphical visualization · R/02_grafici_sd_se.R

# Dependencies: ggplot2
# install.packages("ggplot2")
library(ggplot2)
if (!dir.exists("img")) dir.create("img")

data(iris)
setosa_pl <- iris[iris$Species == "setosa", "Petal.Length"]
n      <- length(setosa_pl); media  <- mean(setosa_pl)
sd_val <- sd(setosa_pl);     se_val <- sd_val / sqrt(n)
df     <- data.frame(x = setosa_pl)
col_sd <- "#C0623C"; col_se <- "#2d6a4f"; col_med <- "#1A1A2E"

# --- GRAPH 1: Distribution + ±SD ---
p1 <- ggplot(df, aes(x = x)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(density)),
                 binwidth=0.1, fill="#a8d8b0", color="white") +
  geom_density(color=col_sd, linewidth=1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept=media, color=col_med, linewidth=1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept=c(media-sd_val, media+sd_val),
             color=col_sd, linetype="dashed", linewidth=1) +
  annotate("segment", x=media-sd_val, xend=media+sd_val, y=2.5, yend=2.5,
           color=col_sd, linewidth=1.2,
           arrow=arrow(ends="both", length=unit(0.2,"cm"))) +
  annotate("text", x=media, y=2.75, fontface="bold", color=col_sd, size=4,
           label=paste0("SD = ±", round(sd_val,3), " cm")) +
  labs(title="Distribution of individual values",
       subtitle="The SD describes the dispersion of individual observations",
       x="Petal length (cm)", y="Density") +
  theme_minimal(base_size=12)

# --- GRAPH 2: SD vs SE as error bars ---
df_err <- data.frame(
  indice = factor(c("Mean ± SD","Mean ± SE"),
                  levels=c("Mean ± SD","Mean ± SE")),
  media  = c(media, media),
  lower  = c(media-sd_val, media-se_val),
  upper  = c(media+sd_val, media+se_val))
p2 <- ggplot(df_err, aes(x=indice, y=media, color=indice,
                           ymin=lower, ymax=upper)) +
  geom_point(size=4) + geom_errorbar(width=0.15, linewidth=1.3) +
  scale_color_manual(values=c("Mean ± SD"=col_sd,"Mean ± SE"=col_se)) +
  labs(title="Visual comparison: SD vs SE",
       subtitle="SE = SD / √n: it is structurally smaller than the SD",
       x=NULL, y="Petal length (cm)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size=12) + theme(legend.position="none")

# --- GRAPH 3: Sampling distribution (bootstrap) ---
set.seed(42)
medie_sim <- replicate(5000, mean(sample(setosa_pl, n, replace=TRUE)))
p3 <- ggplot(data.frame(m=medie_sim), aes(x=m)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y=after_stat(density)), bins=50,
                 fill="#a8d8c0", color="white") +
  geom_density(color=col_se, linewidth=1.3) +
  geom_vline(xintercept=media, color=col_med, linewidth=1.2) +
  geom_vline(xintercept=c(media-se_val, media+se_val),
             color=col_se, linetype="dashed", linewidth=1) +
  labs(title="Sampling distribution of the means (bootstrap, n=5000)",
       subtitle="The SE is the SD of this distribution",
       x="Sample mean (cm)", y="Density") +
  theme_minimal(base_size=12)

# --- GRAPH 4: Comparison between the three species (inferential use) ---
df_sp <- do.call(data.frame,
  aggregate(Petal.Length ~ Species, data=iris,
    FUN=function(x) c(mean=mean(x), se=sd(x)/sqrt(length(x)))))
names(df_sp) <- c("Species","mean","se")
p4 <- ggplot(df_sp, aes(x=Species, y=mean, fill=Species)) +
  geom_col(alpha=0.75, width=0.55) +
  geom_errorbar(aes(ymin=mean-se, ymax=mean+se),
                width=0.22, linewidth=1.1, color="grey25") +
  geom_point(size=3, color="grey20") +
  scale_fill_manual(values=c("setosa"="#2d6a4f",
                              "versicolor"="#C0623C","virginica"="#6b8cba")) +
  labs(title="Mean ± SE of petal length in the three Iris species",
       subtitle="Error bars = ±1 SE — correct use in comparative graphs",
       x=NULL, y="Petal length (cm)", caption="Data: iris (Fisher 1936)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size=12) + theme(legend.position="none")

How to Interpret the Results

Critical reading of the numerical output

Let's refer to the values calculated for Iris setosa, petal length ($n = 50$):

  • SD = 0.174 cm. Individual petals deviate on average by 0.17 cm from the mean. About 68% of I. setosa petals have a length between 1.29 and 1.64 cm. This is data on the biology of the species.
  • SE = 0.025 cm. Our sample mean (1.462 cm) is estimated with an uncertainty of about ±0.025 cm. This tells us how reliable the estimate is, regardless of how much the individuals vary.
  • 95% CI = [1.413 – 1.511] cm. We can state with 95% confidence that the true mean of the I. setosa population falls within this interval.

The ratio $SD/SE = 0.174/0.025 \approx 7 = \sqrt{50}$ is not random: it is always exactly $\sqrt{n}$. If we had 200 observations instead of 50, the SD would remain around 0.174 cm (biological variability doesn't change), but the SE would drop to $0.174/\sqrt{200} \approx 0.012$ cm. A small SD reflects biologically similar individuals; a small SE reflects a lot of collected data.

Interpreting the graphs

Graph 1 shows the distribution of individual values with the SD overlaid. The dashed lines embrace the vast majority of the petals: this is the real biological variability of the species.

Graph 2 compares SD and SE on the same scale. The visual difference is immediate and often surprising for those who have never thought about it: both are measured in centimeters, yet the SE looks almost like a dot. This does not mean the data is "more precise" than it shows: it means that with $n=50$, the mean estimate is very stable.

Graph 3 is the most instructive on a conceptual level. By simulating 5000 random extractions of 50 petals, the distribution of the obtained means is much narrower than the distribution of individual values. Its SD equals the theoretical SE. The SE is literally the "standard deviation of possible sample means" — and that is why it tells us how precise our estimate is.

Graph 4 shows the correct use of the SE in a comparative context. The three species of Iris differ significantly in petal length, and the ±SE bars make this difference evident in a statistically honest way.

The Standard Error in Inferential Practices

Confidence Intervals

The most immediate application of the SE is building the confidence interval (CI) of the mean. For samples of size $n \geq 30$, the normal approximation is often acceptable:

95% Confidence Interval — normal approximation $$CI_{95\%} \approx \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot SE$$

For smaller samples, the coefficient 1.96 must be replaced with the quantile of Student's t distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom (qt(0.975, df = n-1) in R). With $n=50$, you get $t \approx 2.009$, very close to 1.96. With $n=10$, however, $t \approx 2.262$: the interval becomes wider because the uncertainty with little data is greater. Student's t distribution will be the subject of a dedicated lesson.

Comparing Treatments, Species, or Sites

In agronomy and entomology, many research questions boil down to comparing the means of two or more groups: the average yield in plots treated with pesticide A vs pesticide B; the average density of aphids in different crop varieties; the average length of larvae at different rearing temperatures. In all these cases, the SE is the central index.

A useful rule of thumb (but not a substitute for a formal test): if the 95% confidence intervals of two groups do not overlap, the difference is almost certainly significant with $p < 0.05$. If they overlap, significance must be assessed with an appropriate statistical test.

The typical workflow in an experimental statistical evaluation is:

  1. Calculate mean and SE (or 95% CI) for each group or treatment.
  2. Visualize with a bar or dot plot featuring ±SE error bars.
  3. Apply the appropriate statistical test: t-test for two groups, ANOVA for three or more groups.
  4. Report the test value, degrees of freedom, $p$-value, and effect size.

SD or SE in Error Bars: The Practical Choice

This is the most frequent question, and the answer is simple if you are clear about the figure's goal:

  • Bars = SD when the goal is to show the biological variability of the sample or to define reference limits for individual values. Example: describing the weight range of larvae in a colony.
  • Bars = SE or 95% CI when the goal is to compare means between different groups. The error bars in these graphs must express the uncertainty in estimating the mean, not the dispersion of individuals.
  • Always specify in the legend which of the two indices is shown. Simply writing "bars represent error" is insufficient.

Conclusions

SD and SE are both fundamental indices in the statistical practice of life sciences, but they are not interchangeable: they describe two completely different aspects of experimental reality.

The Standard Deviation is a descriptive tool: it reflects the biological variability of the individuals in the sample. Increasing the sample size does not reduce it; it converges towards a population property.

The Standard Error is an inferential tool: it quantifies the uncertainty of our sample mean estimate. Increasing the sample size reduces it proportionally to $1/\sqrt{n}$; it tells us nothing about the biological variability of single individuals.

On the Iris setosa dataset, we have seen this concretely: with $n = 50$, $SD = 0.174$ cm while $SE = 0.025$ cm. The ratio is $\sqrt{50} \approx 7$: structural, not accidental. With 200 observations, the SD would remain ~0.174 cm while the SE would drop to ~0.012 cm.

When I have this goal… I use this index
Describe how much individual subjects vary in the sample SD
Understand if an individual value is "normal" or anomalous SD
Estimate the uncertainty of the sample mean SE
Build a confidence interval for the mean SE
Compare the means of treatments / species / sites SE (or 95% CI)
Error bars in a comparative graph SE — specify in the legend

The final message is simple: the next time you see (or produce) a graph with error bars, always ask — or state — if those bars are SD or SE. It's not bureaucracy: it's scientific honesty.
