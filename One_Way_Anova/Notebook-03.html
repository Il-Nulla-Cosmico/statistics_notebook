One-Way ANOVA

Operational guide: rationale, assumptions, and correct interpretation in R

Movement 01

1. Rationale

The one-way ANOVA is a special case of the general linear model. It compares the means of $k$ groups ($k \ge 3$) defined by a single categorical factor, partitioning the total observed variance into a component explained by the group and a residual one:

Variance Partitioning and F-Statistic $$ SS_{tot} = SS_{between} + SS_{within} $$ $$ F = \frac{MS_{between}}{MS_{within}} = \frac{SS_{between}/(k-1)}{SS_{within}/(N-k)} $$

The crucial starting point is that ANOVA is an omnibus test: the null hypothesis is $H_0: \mu_1=\mu_2=...=\mu_k$. A significant F-value merely indicates that at least one mean differs from the others — not which one, not how many, nor in what direction. Therefore, a post-hoc test is not an optional accessory but a structural necessity if your biological question is "who differs from whom."

Independence between and within groups

These represent two distinct types of violations. Between groups: an observation in group A must not influence those in group B (e.g., placing treatments in the same non-randomized experimental block). Within the group: replicates must be strictly independent of one another. For example, if you measure 30 larvae sourced from only 3 egg masses, your effective $n$ is closer to 3, not 30 (pseudoreplication).

This is the model's non-negotiable requirement. While normality and homoscedasticity can be tested on the observed data, independence cannot — it strictly depends on the upstream experimental design. If violated, the standard errors calculated by the model will be systematically too small, yielding a falsely low p-value, regardless of how impeccably clean the residuals may look.

Criteria for use
Movement 02

2. When to use it

You use it when you have a single factor with $\ge 3$ levels and a continuous or "continuous-like" response variable, alongside independent observations between groups (whether balanced or unbalanced). It serves as the correct alternative to running multiple pairwise t-tests, a practice that drastically inflates the family-wise Type I error rate (e.g., with 5 groups and 10 pairwise comparisons, at $\alpha=0.05$, the probability of obtaining at least one false positive jumps to ~40%).

What does "continuous-like" mean?

ANOVA assumes Gaussian residuals. Some discrete variables behave well enough to be treated as continuous — but this decision cannot be based on intuition; it must be verified. A concrete, quantifiable example is event counting modeled by a Poisson distribution, where the skewness is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$ ($\lambda$ being the mean of the counts).

With a large $\lambda$ (e.g., eggs laid per female, mean ~100 over a range of 40–250), the skewness is very small. The distribution approaches normality, meaning the residuals of an ANOVA can be acceptably Gaussian. With a small $\lambda$ (e.g., emerged parasitoids per host, mean ~1.5 over a range of 0–6, with an abundance of zeros), the skewness is high. The distribution remains heavily skewed with mass concentrated on the lower values. In this scenario, ANOVA is conceptually inadequate regardless of the Shapiro-Wilk test outcome — you need a Poisson or quasi-Poisson GLM.

The operational rule remains single and straightforward: you verify the behavior of the model's residuals, you do not blindly assume it based on the inherent nature of the variable.

(Repeated measures exhibit a different error structure and warrant a separate article — they are not covered here to avoid confusion with the one-way design).

The Assumptions
Movement 03

3. Assumptions

The underlying mathematical model is:

Linear Model $$ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \varepsilon_{ij} \quad \text{where} \quad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2) \text{ i.i.d.} $$

The fundamental requirements are three: independence (discussed above), homoscedasticity (equal variances, checked via Levene's test or Fligner-Killeen as a more robust alternative), and normality of the residuals.

Why the residuals and not the raw data?

The model explicitly assumes the normality of the error, not the raw response. The raw data consists of $\mu + \tau_i$ (the systematic component, which differs per group) plus $\varepsilon_{ij}$ (the noise). Running a Shapiro-Wilk test on aggregated raw data effectively means testing the normality of a mixture of distributions with distinct means. Even with perfectly Gaussian noise within each group, pooling widely separated groups can produce a multimodal distribution, prompting you to reject a model that is actually entirely appropriate.

The residuals $\hat{\varepsilon}_{ij} = y_{ij} - \bar{y}_i$ strip away the group effect: what remains is strictly the error component. That is exactly where the assumption must be verified, because that is where the mathematical model demands it. Running a Shapiro test on Measurement instead of residuals(mod) is a pervasive methodological flaw, not a mere stylistic preference.

With large and balanced sample sizes, ANOVA is reasonably robust to moderate deviations from normality; however, with small and unbalanced samples, it becomes quite fragile.

Limits and Alternatives
Movement 04

4. Common errors and intrinsic limitations

The most prevalent interpretive errors include: testing for normality on the wrong data; executing multiple t-tests instead of a proper post-hoc test; using Tukey's HSD even with heterogeneous variances, where Games-Howell is required; evaluating the p-value without the effect size (thereby conflating statistical significance with biological relevance); misinterpreting a non-significant F-value as "no effect" rather than "insufficient statistical power"; and applying ANOVA to mortality percentages without proper transformation or without transitioning to a binomial GLM.

Regarding intrinsic limitations: ANOVA does not natively handle hierarchical nested structures (a GLMM is required), it is highly sensitive to outliers because it relies on squared deviations, and with severely unbalanced groups, the partitioning of the sums of squares (Type I, II, III) becomes ambiguous.

Alternatives when an assumption fails:

  • Heteroscedasticity → Welch's ANOVA
  • Severe non-normality / ordinal data → Kruskal-Wallis
  • Very small $n$ → Permutation tests
  • Counts / proportions → GLM
  • Nested structure → GLMM
  • Influential outliers → Robust ANOVA on trimmed means (e.g., WRS2::t1way)
The Implementation
Movement 05

5. Practical example in R

R One-Way ANOVA Analysis 
set.seed(42)
data_df <- data.frame(
  weight = c(rnorm(15, 12, 1.5), rnorm(15, 13.2, 1.5),
             rnorm(15, 11.8, 1.5), rnorm(15, 14.0, 1.5)),
  pop = factor(rep(c("A","B","C","D"), each = 15))
)

# Model construction
mod <- aov(weight ~ pop, data = data_df)

# Checking Assumptions (On the residuals!)
shapiro.test(residuals(mod))
car::leveneTest(weight ~ pop, data = data_df)

# Global result (Omnibus test)
summary(mod)

# If variances were found to be heterogeneous:
oneway.test(weight ~ pop, data = data_df, var.equal = FALSE)

# Post-Hoc Test
TukeyHSD(mod)

# Effect Size
effectsize::eta_squared(mod, partial = FALSE)

Reading the numbers
Movement 06

6. Interpreting the results

Every segment of the output must be read in a strict sequence: first the omnibus test, then the post-hoc, and finally the effect size. Skipping a step or reading them out of order is the leading cause of flawed conclusions.

6.1 — The ANOVA table (Omnibus Test)

Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F) pop 3 85.4 28.47 12.63 3.2e-06 *** Residuals 56 126.3 2.26

Checking the Degrees of Freedom (Df): For the factor, it is $k-1$; for the residuals, it is $N-k$. With 4 groups and 15 replicates each ($N=60$), we expect $Df_{pop} = 3$ and $Df_{res} = 56$. If the output displays different numbers (e.g., 54), something went wrong with the data input (unhandled NAs, or incorrectly spelled factor levels). Checking the Df must be done before looking at the p-value.

What you can conclude: "The population factor has a statistically significant effect on pupal weight ($F_{3,56} = 12.63, p < 0.001$)." What you cannot say: "Population D is heavier than A" — the ANOVA table solely indicates that a difference exists somewhere among the groups.

6.2 — The post-hoc (Where the differences lie)

contrast estimate SE df t.ratio p.value A - B -1.20 0.549 56 -2.186 0.1421 A - C 0.15 0.549 56 0.273 0.9930 A - D -2.05 0.549 56 -3.734 0.0027 B - C 1.35 0.549 56 2.459 0.0765 B - D -0.85 0.549 56 -1.548 0.4173 C - D -2.20 0.549 56 -4.007 0.0011

Tukey's HSD compares the groups pairwise, appropriately penalizing the p-values for multiple comparisons. Only A-D and C-D drop below the significance threshold.

A common logical fallacy

The fact that B-D is not significant ($p=0.42$) does not mean that populations B and D have an identical weight — it merely indicates that, given this specific sample size, there is insufficient evidence to confidently assert that they are different. Failing to demonstrate a difference is absolutely not equivalent to proving that a difference does not exist.

6.3 — The Effect Size (How much it matters)

Eta Squared ($\eta^2$) $$ \eta^2 = \frac{SS_{between}}{SS_{total}} = \frac{85.4}{85.4+126.3} \approx 0.40 $$

This metric indicates that the population factor explains approximately 40% of the total variability observed in the data. An effect size acts as a vital counterweight to the p-value: it distinguishes purely statistical significance (e.g., with $N=1000$, even trivial differences yield $p < 0.05$) from genuine biological relevance. (Note: with small sample sizes, $\omega^2$ is generally preferred, as $\eta^2$ tends to overestimate the true effect in the population).

6.4 — Final Checklist

  • Verified assumptions on the residuals (not on the raw data).
  • Reported F, df, and p-value for the omnibus test.
  • Explicitly declared the post-hoc method utilized (e.g., Tukey, Games-Howell).
  • Reported the direction and magnitude of the mean differences.
  • Included the effect size ($\eta^2$ or $\omega^2$) alongside the p-value.
  • Abstained from making absolute claims of equality between non-significant pairs.
